1. Introduction🔗

Imagine a rod with a warm middle and both ends held at zero temperature. Heat flows toward the cold ends, and the temperature falls. The heat equation describes that evolution. An explicit formula gives one solution; an energy estimate proves uniqueness and controls changes in the initial temperature.

On a rod with its ends held at zero temperature, a sine-shaped profile decays without changing shape: u(t,x)=e^{-t}\sin x,\qquad 0\le x\le\pi. Here time and length have been scaled so that the heat equation is u_t=u_{xx}. Differentiating once in time and twice in space gives \begin{aligned} u_t&=-e^{-t}\sin x,\\ u_x&=e^{-t}\cos x,\\ u_{xx}&=-e^{-t}\sin x. \end{aligned} So u_t=u_{xx}: the profile satisfies the heat equation. It also vanishes at both ends of the rod, and at time zero it is \sin x. These are separate facts that together identify the solution and its data.

The first calculation checks u_t-u_{xx}=0.

There is a reason this calculation closes so neatly. The second derivative of \sin x is -\sin x, so substituting u(t,x)=a(t)\sin x reduces the PDE to a'(t)=-a(t). A spatial eigenfunction has turned the evolution into an ordinary differential equation. For the higher mode \sin(kx), with positive integer k, the same calculation gives u_k(t,x)=e^{-k^2t}\sin(kx). Finer oscillations decay faster.

The same initial shape behaves differently on a vibrating string. With zero initial velocity, the wave equation gives u_k(t,x)=\cos(kt)\sin(kx). Both ends stay fixed in each problem. Compare their evolution below.

For the heat mode, try k=3. Its amplitude at time t equals the amplitude of the first mode at time 9t. At the same amplitude, the extra oscillations make the spatial gradient larger and increase the rate of heat dissipation. For the wave mode, displacement and velocity exchange energy as the string vibrates.

The calculation also exposes what has not yet been answered. It gives one solution; uniqueness needs an argument that applies to every solution in a specified class. Replacing the initial sine by a rough temperature profile requires a construction that survives limits. Replacing the rod by all of space leads to the Gaussian kernel, whose initial point mass is understood through convergence against test functions rather than pointwise substitution.

1.1. What a partial differential equation asks🔗

A differential equation relates an unknown function to its derivatives, and a partial differential equation has several independent variables. Schematically a PDE is a relation F\bigl(x,u(x),Du(x),D^2u(x),\ldots,D^ku(x)\bigr)=0 on a domain \Omega. It may be linear or nonlinear in u and its derivatives, stationary or evolutionary. The notation compares values at a point. Yet the questions we care about concern a whole domain or an entire evolution. For the rod, holding the ends at zero lets heat escape; insulating them imposes a zero normal derivative and conserves total heat. The interior equation is the same.

The heat equation u_t-\Delta u=0, the wave equation u_{tt}-c^2\Delta u=0, and the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations \partial_t u +(u\cdot\nabla)u-\nu\Delta u+\nabla p=f,\qquad \operatorname{div}u=0 all need this surrounding information. A domain, function spaces, boundary and initial data, and a meaning for the derivatives turn a differential identity into a PDE problem.

For Poisson's equation, the choice of boundary data appears directly in the problem statement: -\Delta u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. The statement still leaves us work to do. Does g mean an ordinary restriction of a continuous function, or a Sobolev trace? Is f continuous, square-integrable, or a distribution? The answers determine whether we seek pointwise second derivatives or an integral identity. They also determine which estimates are available. An interior equation alone leaves the boundary value free, just as the heat residual alone did not choose the rod's ends.

Hadamard's well-posedness asks for existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on the data. Existence says there is a solution in our chosen class. Uniqueness says the same data cannot produce two solutions in that class. Continuous dependence says that a small change in the data produces a small change in the solution, measured in specified norms. This last requirement is what makes an approximation to the data useful.

We can see it in the rod without leaving the sine functions. Add a small, finer oscillation to the initial temperature: g_\delta(x)=\sin x+\delta\sin(4x). Linearity gives the corresponding solution u_\delta(t,x)=e^{-t}\sin x+\delta e^{-16t}\sin(4x). Subtracting the original solution u_0(t,x)=e^{-t}\sin x leaves just the perturbation. Its size in the L^2(0,\pi) norm is \|u_\delta(t)-u_0(t)\|_{L^2} =|\delta|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}e^{-16t} \le \|g_\delta-g_0\|_{L^2},\qquad t\ge0. Here the L^2 norm measures the square root of the integrated squared temperature error. Heat flow damps that error; the fine oscillation disappears even faster than the original profile. The formula constructs solutions for these particular data. The energy argument below will explain why the same stability estimate applies much more generally, and why it also gives uniqueness when the initial difference is zero.

Trying to recover the past temperature reverses this advantage. Fix T>0. The final profiles e^{-n^2T}\sin(nx) tend to zero in L^2 as n\to\infty, but their initial profiles \sin(nx) all have norm \sqrt{\pi/2}. Arbitrarily small errors at the final time can therefore hide a substantial initial oscillation. Even on these smooth, exactly solvable data, the inverse heat map is not continuous in L^2. Well-posedness belongs to an equation together with its data, direction of evolution, solution class, and topology.

For linear second-order equations, the principal part explains much of this behavior. Elliptic equations describe equilibrium. Harmonic functions obey mean-value identities and maximum principles, so boundary values constrain the interior. Divergence-form operators -\operatorname{div}(A(x)\nabla u)+b(x)\cdot\nabla u+c(x)u=f are particularly suited to integration by parts: their weak formulations expose bilinear forms whose coercivity can give existence and uniqueness. Parabolic equations describe diffusion. The decay of the higher sine modes is one manifestation of smoothing; spreading a localized pulse with a Gaussian is another. Hyperbolic equations describe propagation along characteristics, with finite speed in the wave equation.

The three panels show a steady harmonic saddle, a spreading heat pulse, and a traveling wave. The distinction matters when choosing a proof: a maximum principle controls an equilibrium through its boundary, a diffusion estimate controls smoothing, and characteristics follow information as it moves. Nonlinearity can change the solution class itself. A smooth initial profile for a conservation law may develop a shock. The distributional equation continues to make sense, but entropy inequalities are needed to select an admissible solution. Hamilton–Jacobi equations instead use viscosity solutions, defined through comparison with touching test functions.

Fluid mechanics brings these methods together. Euler describes inviscid transport constrained by incompressibility; Navier–Stokes adds viscous diffusion. Pressure is recovered through an elliptic equation even though velocity evolves in time. Leray's global weak solutions to three-dimensional Navier–Stokes supply energy control in a weak solution class. Classical regularity and uniqueness require further arguments in a specified class.

1.2. How the subject acquired its language🔗

The higher heat modes suggest a way to handle more general initial data: expand the temperature in a sine series and evolve each coefficient. For a finite sum, differentiation and addition commute and the calculation is immediate. For an infinite series, convergence, differentiation, and recovery of the initial function become part of the problem. This is an old source of new analysis.

D'Alembert's wave equation already raised the question: its solutions involve arbitrary traveling profiles, while the eighteenth-century understanding of a function was tied to analytic expressions, and Euler, d'Alembert, and the Bernoullis disagreed over which shapes could count as initial data. Fourier's trigonometric series represented rough initial temperatures and evolved them by damping high frequencies long before convergence and termwise differentiation were understood. These two equations pushed analysis toward the arbitrary function, rigorous integration, and Fourier analysis.

Potential theory exposed a different difficulty. Dirichlet's principle related a harmonic function with prescribed boundary values to a minimizer of an energy. Riemann used the principle successfully, but Weierstrass objected to the existence argument: an energy can have an infimum without attaining it. Choosing functions whose energies approach the best possible value does not yet produce a best function. Completeness, compactness, and lower semicontinuity explain when that limiting step works. We will meet the same issue when constructing weak solutions from approximations.

Hilbert-space methods gave linear PDE an abstract setting, but limits still need not preserve classical derivatives. Sobolev's weak derivatives made it possible to complete smooth functions in norms controlling both values and derivatives. Schwartz's distributions gave differentiation a setting in which it is always defined and continuous. Leray's Navier–Stokes construction combined a weak formulation with energy control to obtain global solutions, without thereby obtaining global smoothness or uniqueness. This remains a productive way to approach an equation: choose a solution concept stable under the estimates, construct a solution there, and then investigate its regularity. Entropy and viscosity solutions make different choices because shocks and nondifferentiable value functions present different obstacles.

Computer-assisted analysis creates another need for a usable mathematical object. We may be able to inspect the idea of an estimate without reading every one of its arithmetic steps. A certificate records data from which a checker can prove the claimed identity or bound. The search for it can use heuristics; the accepted result has a precise mathematical meaning. PDEAtlas uses such certificates alongside weak derivatives and energy estimates, so that a long calculation can enter an argument through an ordinary theorem.

1.3. Solution concepts🔗

Suppose the initial temperature on the rod has a jump. Finite sine sums still give smooth approximate evolutions, but their limit at time zero need not be differentiable. Requiring the same pointwise calculus at every stage would exclude the datum we wanted to study. We need a meaning of differentiation that makes sense before smoothing has occurred.

Our sine mode is a classical solution: all derivatives in the equation exist in the ordinary sense. Classical regularity is often the conclusion we hope to recover from an existence proof. It can be too much to demand of an approximation limit, and some nonlinear equations destroy it during the evolution.

A distributional solution tests the equation against smooth compactly supported functions and moves derivatives by integration by parts. If u and f are locally integrable, -\Delta u=f in distributions means \int_\Omega u\,(-\Delta\varphi) =\int_\Omega f\,\varphi \qquad \text{for every }\varphi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega). The unknown no longer needs two pointwise derivatives. The test function supplies them, and its compact support removes the boundary terms. The Heaviside step makes the change tangible: if H(x) is zero to the left of the origin and one to the right, then \langle H',\varphi\rangle =-\int_0^\infty\varphi'(x)\,dx =\varphi(0). It has no classical derivative at the origin, but its distributional derivative is the Dirac mass.

Sobolev weak derivatives impose an additional requirement: the distributional derivative must be represented by an L^p function. Thus W^{1,p}(\Omega) controls the integrability of both the function and its first weak derivatives. The step function illustrates the distinction: its Dirac derivative is a distribution, but is not an L^p function across the jump. Distributional and Sobolev derivatives solve related problems with different amounts of regularity.

For the elliptic equation -\operatorname{div}(A\nabla u)=f, moving one derivative onto a test function gives \int_\Omega A\nabla u\cdot\nabla v =\int_\Omega f\,v \qquad\text{for every admissible test function }v. The left side is a bilinear form in u and v. This lets Lax–Milgram apply once the form is bounded and coercive and the right side is a continuous functional. Monotone operators, compactness, and Fredholm theory extend this approach to other equations. The zero-boundary space H_0^1(\Omega) encodes a homogeneous Dirichlet condition by closure of compactly supported smooth functions. A trace theorem, when available on the domain, relates that condition to the boundary values of the function.

Variational formulations express the equation as an identity against test functions. When an energy is available, its first variation can yield this identity. For example, Dirichlet's principle seeks a minimizer of E(u)=\frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2-\int_\Omega fu. For an admissible variation v, expand E(u+\varepsilon v) and differentiate at \varepsilon=0. At a minimizer the result is 0=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}E(u+\varepsilon v)\right|_{\varepsilon=0} =\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-\int_\Omega fv. This is the weak Poisson equation. The calculation explains what a minimizer would satisfy. To show that a minimizer exists, we need coercivity to bound a minimizing sequence, weak compactness to extract a limit, weak closedness to keep the limit admissible, and weak lower semicontinuity to keep its energy minimal. The first variation and the existence argument answer different parts of Dirichlet's principle.

Time adds a further issue. For weak heat flow, the spatial energy naturally controls u in L^2(0,T;V), while the equation controls u_t in L^2(0,T;V^*). A typical choice is V=H_0^1(\Omega) and H=L^2(\Omega), with the dense continuous inclusions V\hookrightarrow H\hookrightarrow V^* forming a Gelfand triple. The time derivative is a functional on spatial test functions; it need not itself lie in H.

An L^2 class in time has no preferred value at time zero. Lions–Magenes theory recovers an H-continuous representative and an energy identity from the two time-space bounds. This is how initial data can become meaningful again after an existence argument has passed through almost-everywhere classes.

Fluid mechanics adds named classes because their extra conditions do mathematical work. A Leray–Hopf solution satisfies the weak Navier–Stokes equations together with integrability, weak continuity, and an energy inequality; a suitable weak solution carries the local energy inequality needed for partial regularity; a classical solution has enough regularity for pointwise calculus. A theorem about one class says nothing about another until a regularity or comparison argument connects them.

For entropy solutions, an inequality selects among distributional solutions of a nonlinear conservation law. For viscosity solutions, how smooth test functions touch a possibly nondifferentiable function from above or below is what matters.

1.4. Formalization in Lean🔗

For the sine mode, "the boundary term vanishes" can be checked by evaluating the sine at zero and \pi. For a weak solution in a zero-boundary Sobolev space, the same words refer to approximation or traces. The diagram shows why moving between solution concepts needs a mathematical argument: an integration-by-parts step moves in one direction, a regularity theorem may permit the reverse direction, and minimizing an energy introduces its own conditions.

In several variables the Fréchet derivative is a continuous linear map; applying it to a coordinate direction gives a partial derivative. In one dimension we use the ordinary scalar derivative. Building these operations from Mathlib's definitions lets a coordinate calculation feed into theorems about linear maps and norms. The mathlib4 discussion Partial derivatives explains why coordinates, differentiation along a line, and Fréchet differentiation need to be distinguished. Regularity hypotheses tell us when we may move between them.

There is a less familiar consequence of using these definitions. Lean functions are total: division, logarithms, square roots, and differentiation return values even outside the domain where a textbook uses their usual rules. We can write \Delta u without first proving that u is twice differentiable. To interpret it as a classical Laplacian, we must supply the regularity that justifies the two differentiations. Similarly, the rational identity in the opening notebook keeps its nonzero-denominator assumption. The ability to write an expression does not grant the calculus rule we want to apply to it.

Equality needs comparable care. A function can change on a set of measure zero without changing its L^p class or the distribution it represents. Such a change can still alter a pointwise boundary value. To recover a pointwise statement from an almost-everywhere one, we need further structure, such as continuous representatives. The time-continuity theorem for weak heat flow is one example of this work, not a matter of changing notation.

Boundary regularity presents another choice. "Let \Omega be smooth" can mean many things depending on the theorem: extension, trace, surface measure, Green's identity, elliptic estimates, and boundary flattening need different geometric assumptions. Where a proof uses a chart, a cutoff, a flattening map, a Jacobian estimate, or an orientation, PDEAtlas can take that object and its properties as explicit input. This makes a local analytic argument usable before every geometric construction is available. It also leaves an obligation visible: a theorem assuming those data is not yet a construction of them from a Lipschitz or smooth boundary.

1.4.1. Lean, Mathlib, and the trusted core🔗

Lean is both a programming language and an interactive theorem prover. Its logic identifies propositions with types and proofs with terms: a statement such as \forall x\in\Omega,\; \Delta u(x)=f(x) is a type, and a proof is a term of that type. A tactic helps construct the term; the kernel checks it against the statement. This separation lets us use elaborate search procedures without making their every decision part of the mathematical justification. A mistaken candidate still needs to pass the checker.

Mathlib supplies the shared objects: real and complex numbers, measures and Bochner integrals, normed spaces and continuous linear maps, derivatives and distributions. Its Fourier analysis, weak topologies, spectral results, and compact operators support the later arguments. Reusing these definitions matters when two proofs meet. The derivative returned by the CAS and the derivative used by an energy theorem should describe the same operation; otherwise every application would need another equivalence proof. The expression language used for computation therefore comes with theorems connecting its interpretation to ordinary Mathlib functions.

CAS, bare cega, ordinary proofs, and the numerical checks are checked in the kernel. Users can explicitly opt into cega [fast] for closed finite certificates and polynomial interval evaluation; under the fast build profile these checks additionally trust Lean's compiler and native evaluation, and the results that use them inherit the generated native axioms. The default build profile checks them in the kernel. If these routes cannot certify the goal, the tactic falls back to kernel CEGA; the exponential, logarithmic, and other proof-bearing planner routes remain unchanged.

The kernel checks the implication we stated, relative to the foundations and assumptions it uses. Choosing that statement is still our responsibility. A proof of a residual identity cannot detect that the author meant to ask for the initial-boundary value problem. Nor can it decide whether an equation models a particular fluid or whether the prose describes a cited paper correctly.

We follow Evans (2010) from classical formulas through Sobolev spaces to weak equations, variational methods, conservation laws, and Hamilton–Jacobi theory. PDE problem records distinguish the residual, initial data, boundary data, and regularity of a solution.

1.4.2. From a paper theorem to a Lean theorem🔗

Take the weak Poisson problem on a bounded open domain \Omega\subset\mathbb R^n, n\ge1, with zero boundary data. For f\in L^2(\Omega), choose V=H_0^1(\Omega) and set a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v,\qquad F(v)=\int_\Omega fv. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds both expressions. Poincaré's inequality makes the gradient control the full H^1 norm on V, giving coercivity of a and continuity of F. Lax–Milgram then supplies a unique u\in V with a(u,v)=F(v) for every v\in V. Unfolding the two definitions gives exactly the weak equation.

Lax–Milgram applies to a bounded coercive form on any real Hilbert space. The Poisson argument verifies those hypotheses for the gradient form. A coefficient matrix A changes the boundedness and coercivity estimates, but leaves the abstract theorem available. A claim of classical regularity adds another argument beyond the weak existence result.

A paper can distribute these assumptions over a chapter. The Lean statement has to gather the ambient spaces, measures, exponents, coefficient and domain conditions, and solution class. An interior regularity theorem, for example, has no boundary conclusion, although both results go by the name elliptic regularity.

1.4.3. Five recurring proof patterns🔗

An explicit solution gives us something to calculate. To prove uniqueness, handle rough data, or solve an equation without a formula, we need arguments that use less information about the unknown. The following patterns explain how the analysis grows beyond the opening example.

Explicit formulas and residuals. On all of \mathbb R^n, the heat kernel is \Gamma(x,t) =\frac{1}{(4\pi t)^{n/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{|x|^2}{4t}\right), \qquad t>0, and differentiation gives the heat equation at positive time. The spatial and time derivatives produce the same factor: \Delta\Gamma(x,t) =\left(\frac{|x|^2}{4t^2}-\frac{n}{2t}\right)\Gamma(x,t) =\partial_t\Gamma(x,t). Near the peak this factor is negative, so the temperature falls there as the pulse spreads. The condition t>0 is essential to the calculation because the formula divides by t.

The new issue is the initial datum. The kernel has mass one at every positive time, but its mass concentrates at the origin as t\downarrow0. Its initial value is a Dirac mass in the distributional sense. For bounded, continuous g, convolution \Gamma(\cdot,t)*g tends to g locally uniformly. To prove this, normalization accounts for the constant part of g; continuity controls its variation near a point; and the Gaussian tail controls the integral far away. This approximation-of-the-identity argument connects the classical formula at positive time with its initial data. Differentiating the formula does not perform that limiting argument.

Harmonic kernels, traveling waves, similarity profiles, shear flows, and rotating solutions share this pattern: establish regularity, calculate the residual, and identify the data or normalization. A singular formula can make the last step the most revealing. The fundamental solution of the Laplacian is harmonic away from its pole, yet it has a distributional point source there. A zero classical residual on the punctured domain misses that source. Flux and integration by parts recover it. This is another place where the classical and distributional views supply different parts of the answer.

Energy estimates and uniqueness. Multiply the heat equation by u, integrate, and use the zero boundary value to obtain \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\|u(t)\|_{L^2}^2 +\|\nabla u(t)\|_{L^2}^2=0, under regularity assumptions that justify the differentiation and integration by parts. For the sine mode on (0,\pi), the energy is \tfrac12\|u(t)\|_{L^2}^2=\tfrac\pi4e^{-2t} and the dissipation is \|u_x(t)\|_{L^2}^2=\tfrac\pi2e^{-2t}. The energy derivative is the negative of the dissipation, as the identity predicts. The useful feature of the energy identity is that its proof does not require knowing the formula for u.

Apply it to the difference of two heat solutions with the same data. The difference begins with zero energy; dissipation prevents the energy from increasing; nonnegativity forces it to stay zero. This proves uniqueness in a class where the argument is justified. For weak solutions, the Gelfand triple and Lions–Magenes identity supply the time pairing, while the zero-boundary space supplies the integration-by-parts step.

For the wave equation the energy includes velocity and spatial gradient. For Navier–Stokes the transport term formally cancels, leaving viscous dissipation; making that cancellation valid requires the appropriate integrability and boundary behavior. A weak solution may give only an energy inequality, which supports different conclusions from an equality. Energy also helps construct solutions. In Galerkin approximation we solve finite-dimensional equations first, then use bounds independent of the dimension to obtain a limit. Grönwall, Poincaré, and Sobolev inequalities turn these bounds into stability, uniqueness, or compactness estimates.

Compactness and passage to a limit. A finite sine sum can be differentiated term by term. Passing to an infinite expansion requires control independent of the number of terms. The same need appears with smoother, truncated, or regularized approximations u_m. Bounds in a reflexive Banach space give weakly convergent subsequences, and continuous linear functionals pass to the limit. This is why weak formulations are well suited to construction.

Nonlinear terms expose what weak convergence loses. On (0,2\pi), the sequence \sin(mx) converges weakly to zero in L^2, while \sin^2(mx)=\frac{1-\cos(2mx)}{2} \rightharpoonup\frac12. Squaring and taking a weak limit do not commute. Strong compactness, monotonicity, compensated structure, or a separate identification argument is needed to handle such terms. In an evolution problem, spatial compactness alone also leaves room for rapid oscillation in time. Aubin–Lions–Simon estimates combine a compact spatial embedding with a bound on time variation to give strong convergence in an intermediate space. PDEAtlas proves the version for a Hilbert triple, with square-integrable bounds in time.

The initial condition is still a separate step. Convergence on an open time interval does not determine a value at its endpoint. Lions–Magenes continuity or appropriately chosen time test functions recover that value, which is why a complete Galerkin theorem includes more than an interior weak equation.

Coercivity, minimization, and operator methods. The weak Poisson argument has reduced the PDE to a(u,v)=F(v) for all v\in V. Boundedness and coercivity of the bilinear form on a real Hilbert space, together with continuity of F, give the unique solution through Lax–Milgram. The direct method gives another route when an energy is available: bound a minimizing sequence, keep its weak limit in the admissible set, and use weak lower semicontinuity to obtain a minimum. Strict convexity gives uniqueness, and an admissible first variation gives the Euler–Lagrange equation.

Other operator methods preserve this separation between an abstract theorem and the PDE estimates that make it applicable. Minty–Browder arguments use monotonicity to identify weak limits of nonlinear operators. Writing an equation as u=T(u) can permit a Banach contraction argument for a short time or small data, or a Schauder argument using continuity and compactness. Fredholm theory describes solvability through the kernel of an operator and its adjoint when unconditional invertibility is unavailable. Each method asks for properties of the spaces and operators that the PDE proof must establish.

Comparison, characteristics, and admissibility. Order provides a different kind of control. Maximum principles and comparison principles turn boundary or data inequalities into bounds on the solution. They can give positivity, barriers, and uniqueness, with coefficient signs and boundary assumptions determining the precise result. For constant-velocity transport, the solution is carried along x(t)=x_0+tb, giving u(x,t)=u_0(x-tb). The traveling-wave panel showed this preservation of shape. In a quasilinear equation, the speed can depend on the solution, so characteristics may intersect and the classical construction can break down.

The weak conservation law allows discontinuities but constrains their motion through the Rankine–Hugoniot relation. Entropy inequalities impose the dissipation direction that selects admissible shocks. Viscosity sub- and supersolutions instead express comparison through touching test functions. In each case the extra condition supplies information that the distributional equation alone would leave undecided.

1.5. Verified symbolic and numerical computation🔗

The heat-mode calculation asks a computer to do something quite specific: differentiate a product of elementary functions, then recognize that the two resulting expressions agree. The weak existence argument asks for something else: quantify over a space of functions and pass to limits. We want the first calculation to produce a theorem that the second kind of argument can use. That requirement explains both the expression language of the CAS and its connection to Mathlib.

The same division between a candidate calculation and a checked conclusion appears in Lewis and Wu's Lean–Mathematica interface Lewis and Wu (2021). Hex supplies verified computational algebra, with Mathlib correspondence layers where available contributors (2026). Our task here is to connect calculations to the analytic functions used by a PDE proof: a normalized derivative must agree with Mathlib's derivative, and an interval must contain the actual real expression.

An arbitrary function \mathbb R\to\mathbb R need not come with a formula that a program can inspect. PDEAtlas therefore represents a limited language of formulas as data: variables, constants, arithmetic, powers, trigonometric functions, exponentials, logarithms, and square roots. A program can walk the resulting expression tree, apply the product or chain rule at each node, and normalize the result. A soundness theorem proves that the transformed tree has the claimed meaning as a real function. This is a deep embedding: syntax is an object we can compute with, and its interpretation is tied to the ordinary mathematics by a proof. Users write ordinary Lean notation; the conversion to a tree happens internally.

For the heat mode, the final task is to connect the computed derivatives to the analytic heat residual. The residual bridge theorems supply this connection. Gradient and Laplacian calculations must preserve the coordinate order: differentiating in time and differentiating in space are different operations even when both coordinates are real numbers. The same distinction appears in a smaller example. Treat y as an independent parameter and differentiate \sin(xy) with respect to x:

The inspection command displays a result; the tactic must close the goal with a proof. Exact simplification similarly produces an equality theorem. Integration searches for a supported antiderivative, then differentiates the candidate and checks that its difference from the integrand normalizes to zero. Thus finding an antiderivative and accepting it can use different procedures. A complicated search still ends with a derivative calculation whose meaning is proved.

Domain conditions travel with these calculations. The notebook's rational identity cancels x^2-1, so it assumes x^2-1\ne0. Cancelling x/x needs x\ne0; replacing \exp(\log x) by x needs x>0. The last condition is stronger than differentiability of Mathlib's real logarithm, which holds at every nonzero real input. A classical square-root derivative requires a positive argument. Total functions make these distinctions easy to miss on the page: an expression still has a value where a proposed rule is invalid. The CAS must prove the condition for the particular rule it uses. In the Gaussian calculation, this is why positive time accompanies the formula throughout the proof.

The exact layer includes normalization, differentiation, selected antiderivative search, polynomial reasoning, and real-root certificates. Its expressions form a fixed grammar of real elementary functions, and its integrator searches a finite family of antiderivative forms. A goal outside the grammar, or one that the search cannot settle, is left unchanged and the tactic reports an error.

For explicit expressions, the checked tactics let the proof concentrate on how a calculation is used. Abstract functions, symbolic dimension, norms and inner products, sums, piecewise formulas, and vector-valued derivatives can still require direct Mathlib arguments. This division is useful in the Gaussian example: a concrete coordinate residual is a good CAS calculation, while normalization and convergence to the initial datum require analysis. Like Mathlib's reflected algebra and arithmetic tactics, the CAS proves statements about Mathlib's own objects. Its additional expression and certificate machinery handles the continuous operations needed here.

1.5.1. Certified numerical work🔗

The notebook's third cell bounds x/(1+x) for every x\in[0,1]. Ordinary interval arithmetic already gives a valid enclosure: the numerator lies in [0,1] and the denominator in [1,2], so their quotient lies in [0,1]. But treating those ranges independently loses information. A numerator near one has a denominator near two. Differentiation recovers the relationship: \frac{d}{dx}\frac{x}{1+x}=\frac{1}{(1+x)^2}>0 \qquad (0\le x\le1). Monotonicity reduces the sharp bound to the two endpoints, giving 0\le x/(1+x)\le1/2. The notebook proves that bound over the entire interval. This explains why a numerical checker benefits from symbolic calculus: algebra and derivative information can sharpen a bound that raw interval operations make too wide.

CEGA (Certified Enclosures, Guarantees, and Approximations) states such bounds as theorems about ordinary real expressions. An interval enclosure covers every input in a rational box. A bound certificate proves an upper or absolute bound. An approximation certificate pairs an approximating function with a uniform error theorem. Taylor and quadrature certificates include the hypotheses and remainder estimates needed for their inequalities. Each conclusion holds on the whole domain, which no finite sample of points could establish.

The basic interval backend uses exact rational endpoints and proved inclusion rules for arithmetic and supported transcendental functions. A search may use monotonicity, derivative bounds, sparse polynomial structure, or subdivision to obtain a sufficiently narrow interval. Scalar Taylor certificates retain a polynomial and a certified uniform remainder on each interval. Quadrature proofs use exactness on polynomial pieces and derivative bounds to control the integral error. Root isolation, interval Newton, Krawczyk-style arguments, and validated linear algebra use the same separation between proposing data and checking its consequence. A search proposes a bracket, an approximate inverse, or a subdivision. A Lean checker proves what follows from those data. Search choices affect sharpness and runtime; they cannot change the statement to be established. If the expression, domain conditions, or required certificate cannot be handled within the search budget, the proof-producing interface fails. Displaying an intermediate result does not discharge the goal.

To see what a residual certificate could contribute to a PDE argument, return to the rod and suppose we approximate e^{-t} by a continuously differentiable function q(t) with q(0)=1. The candidate \widetilde u(t,x)=q(t)\sin x has the exact boundary and initial data, but its heat residual is r(t,x)=\widetilde u_t-\widetilde u_{xx} =\bigl(q'(t)+q(t)\bigr)\sin x. For T>0 and \varepsilon\ge0, suppose a certificate proves |q'(t)+q(t)|\le\varepsilon on [0,T]. This is a bound on a particular real expression, with its time interval specified. It gives \|r(t)\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}^2\le(\pi/2)\varepsilon^2, using the integral of \sin^2 x on the rod.

Now compare the candidate with the exact heat solution and write e=\widetilde u-u. The error has zero initial and boundary data and satisfies e_t-e_{xx}=r. The energy calculation gives \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\|e(t)\|_{L^2}^2+\|e_x(t)\|_{L^2}^2 =\int_0^\pi e(t,x)r(t,x)\,dx \le\frac12\|e(t)\|_{L^2}^2+\frac12\|r(t)\|_{L^2}^2. Dropping the nonnegative dissipation and applying Grönwall yields \|e(t)\|_{L^2(0,\pi)}^2 \le\frac\pi2\varepsilon^2(e^t-1),\qquad 0\le t\le T. The residual bound has become a solution-error bound because the heat equation supplies the stability argument. For a nonlinear equation, the corresponding estimate may require smallness, weights, or operator bounds, and proving those conditions can be most of the work. A certificate is useful precisely when its conclusion matches an input of that analytic argument.

1.6. Sources🔗

Evans (2010) is the principal reference because it connects the core equation families to the functional analysis needed to study them. We contacted Evans about following his book, and he was excited about the project. Results that track it carry source locators, which refer to the AMS second edition (2010). The source notes record the contents of that edition and the pages of selected passages. The explanations here are written for this project, not copied from the book.

The time-space arguments above draw on Simon (1986) for compactness and Lions and Magenes (1972) for recovery of time continuity. Marcus and Mizel (1972) supplies the context for absolute continuity on lines and Sobolev composition. The abstract existence methods lead to Banach (1922), Schauder (1930), Minty (1962), and Browder (1963) for fixed points, monotonicity, and nonlinear variational arguments. Crandall and Lions (1983) is the reference for viscosity solutions.

For fluids, the choice of solution class is particularly important. Leray (1934) is the historical source for global weak Navier–Stokes solutions; Caffarelli et al. (1982) studies partial regularity of suitable weak solutions. Serrin (1962) and Escauriaza et al. (2003) give interior and endpoint regularity criteria. Majda and Bertozzi (2001) and Temam (2001) develop the vorticity and functional frameworks, Ukhovskii and Yudovich (1968) the axisymmetric setting, and Chen and Hou (2022) the nearly self-similar Boussinesq and Euler analysis. Fefferman's official description fixes the Clay problem under discussion, and Wiedemann is the source for weak incompressible Euler solutions.

The computation chapter gives the references behind its algorithms: Moore and Neumaier on interval analysis, Berz and Makino on Taylor models, Risch and Bronstein on symbolic integration, and Sturm's theorem for real-root certificates.

Blowup for the De Gregorio model follows a different kind of singularity: vorticity grows while its physical support expands along the real line.
