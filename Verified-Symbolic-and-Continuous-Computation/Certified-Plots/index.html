10.12. Certified Plots🔗

The Gaussian kernel, traveling sine, and Burgers profile have already served as examples of PDE identities. We return to them here to ask a different question: which values can each function take throughout a small spatial interval? The enclosure machinery answers that question with a theorem for each panel. The horizontal axis is cut into forty rational panels, and a theorem places each function between two rational endpoints throughout every panel. The theorem is proved by the cega tactic from an interval certificate and checked by Lean's kernel when the site is built. What is drawn is exactly the union of those certified boxes: three explicit solutions from the parabolic and hyperbolic chapters, as the calculator certified them.

How to read a plot. Each colour is one value of the time parameter t. Each shaded panel is one theorem: its horizontal extent is the panel [a, b] of the x-axis and its vertical extent is the certified range [\ell, h], so the true curve runs inside the shaded strip. Each strip covers the function's variation over the panel and may also include overestimation from the enclosure calculation. Its endpoints are rounded outward to six decimal places so that the statements stay readable.

Selecting a panel opens its row in the theorem listing under the plot. Each row gives the panel, the certified range, and the full Lean statement, of the form

EnclosedOn (fun p : ℝ^1 ↦ Real.sin (p 0 - 1))
  {p | 0 ≤ p 0 ∧ p 0 ≤ 3/20} (-841519/1000000) (-375639/500000)

read "for every point p of the one-dimensional Euclidean space with 0 \le p_0 \le 3/20, the value \sin(p_0 - 1) lies between the two rationals". The theorem name links to its line in the generated module, which the Blueprint build compiles. The generator asks the calculator for every certified range before writing the theorems, and it checks that the drawing and the theorems list the same panels and bounds.

The first plot is the one-dimensional heat kernel \Phi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\, e^{-x^2/(4t)} at t = 1/4, 1/2 and 1 on x \in [-3, 3]. Its total mass is one at every time, so the curve spreads and flattens as t grows. The panels are narrow strips near the tails, where the kernel is nearly flat, and taller around the flanks of the peak. The certificates handle the real power (4\pi t)^{-1/2}, the exponential and the constant \pi together; the Gaussian-kernel section of the parabolic chapter proves the kernel's properties as theorems and the textbook coverage map locates them in Evans's §2.3.

The second plot is d'Alembert's traveling sine wave u(x, t) = \sin(x - t) at t = 1/2, 1 and 3/2 on x \in [-3, 3]. The wave moves to the right with unit speed, so the three curves are translates of one another by one half. The function is the one whose wave-equation residual is checked in the wave and transport example.

The third plot is the self-similar Burgers profile u(x, t) = x / t at t = 1/2, 1 and 2 on x \in [-1, 1]. This is the formula used inside a rarefaction fan. The full Riemann solution restricts it to the region between the rays determined by the two states and joins it to constant values outside. The plot encloses only the fan formula, on a fixed window, without the outer states. The profile is increasing and linear in x on every panel, so its exact range is determined by its endpoint values. The strips show how the fan opens with time: the same window of x covers a smaller range of velocities at later times.
